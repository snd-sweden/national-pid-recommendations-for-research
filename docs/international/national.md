# National actors

_Last updated: 2026-09-30_

## EU member states

The Dutch roadmap from 2022 was written by a working group for the Dutch national PID advisory board as a first step towards a national roadmap[@rep-NationalPID-22]. For registering and reporting research output and making it reusable and reproducible, it recommends wider adoption of ORCID, support for organisation identifiers and grant DOIs, DOIs for research software, and a taskforce to monitor emerging PIDs.

Finland's roadmap was produced by PID Forum Finland and published in 2023 with minor updates in 2024[@nationalroadmappersistent-24]. It proposes actions for building trust and cooperation, organising the national infrastructure and increasing awareness and competence, but aims neither to centralise the infrastructure nor to create new PID systems. In pilots in the national research information service Research.fi, the RAiD metadata schema has served as the basis for a national data model for research projects, and DataCite's PIDGraph concept has been used to add links between research outputs[@jrnl-PilotingUseRAiD-24; @jrnl-PilotingUsePIDGraph-24].

Ireland's roadmap from 2024 was produced in response to the National Action Plan for Open Research 2022–2030[@rep-InteroperabilityOpenness-24]. It gives recommendations with actions up to 2030, and builds on a cost-benefit analysis of priority PIDs (ORCID, RAiD, ROR, DOIs for outputs and grants) that estimated a net benefit of 1.6 million euro over five years, including the cost of a central PID support service[@rep-EfficiencyInsight-24].

In the Czech Republic, work towards a national PID strategy was at an early stage in 2023, and planned as part of a national open science strategy[@rep-RDANational-23]. With EU funding, a National PID Centre has been set up, which runs national ORCID and DataCite consortia. A cost-benefit analysis from 2024 estimates that reusing PID metadata for publications, grants and projects could save up to 7,207 person days a year, and that central support lowers the adoption level at which savings exceed costs from just under 60% to 50–55%[@rep-CostbenefitAnalysis-24]. It recommends a target of 80% coverage for the five priority PIDs, and PID integration in shared national systems, such as the national research information system, so that smaller organisations also benefit. The analysis is to be revisited in 2028.

France's recommendations from 2025 were published as part of the research ministry's roadmap on data for simplification and research management, which follows the metadata input principle of "tell us once"[@rep-RecommendationsAdoption-25]. They prioritise ORCID iDs for people, Crossref DOIs for publications, DataCite DOIs for data, SWHID for software and ROR IDs for research organisations, with a national organisation registry feeding ROR, and recommend a national deployment of RAiD. They favour PID services run by non-profit organisations with community governance, and call for a national PID governance body, sustainable funding and a risk analysis of the dependence on PID infrastructures, including digital sovereignty.

Germany's roadmap from 2026 was produced by PID Network Deutschland and covers both research and cultural heritage institutions[@rep-PIDRoadmap-26]. It recommends measures for the next five years on entity coverage, metadata quality, technical infrastructure, governance and competence development, but deliberately does not specify particular PID systems. The roadmap is to be translated into a binding national PID action plan.

## United Kingdom

The UK roadmap was written for Jisc in 2019, in response to a recommendation that Jisc should lead on selecting and promoting identifiers for open access to research[@rep-DevelopingPersistent-19]. Based on workshops with funders, publishers, infrastructure providers and research institutions, it prioritised PIDs for publications, people, organisations, grants and projects, and proposed a national PID consortium building on the Jisc ORCID consortium. A case for investment later described three components of a national approach: consortia for access to key PIDs, a national coordination body, the Research Identifier National Coordinating Committee (RINCC), and a dedicated support team[@rep-CaseInvestment-22]. A cost-benefit analysis estimated that such a PID support network, promoting five priority PIDs (DOIs for outputs and grants, ORCID iDs, RAiD and ROR IDs), would save the higher education sector over £45 million over five years[@rep-RevisedCostbenefit-22]. Follow-up work also produced PID-optimised workflows for funding, institutional research management, article publication and research data, which map each step of these processes to the PIDs involved and show how information collected once can be reused across the research lifecycle[@pidoptimisedworkflowsvision-22]. The workflows rely mainly on the five priority PIDs, complemented by other identifiers where needed, such as Handles, ARKs and RRIDs in research data workflows.

## Canada

Canada's work started with a 2022 report for the Canadian Persistent Identifier Advisory Committee (CPIDAC) by MoreBrains Cooperative, based on interviews and workshops with 40 stakeholders, which recommended building a broad coalition, articulating the value of PIDs for each stakeholder group, and setting out a roadmap for a national strategy with a core set of PIDs[@rep-NationalPID-22a]. The strategy itself has since been developed by CPIDAC. In its version from March 2026, it endorses ORCID iDs for people, DOIs for research outputs and ROR IDs for organisations, while DOIs for grants and RAiD for projects are under consideration[@rep-CanadianPID-26]. National ORCID and DataCite consortia lower the costs for institutions, and governance is deliberately light, as the committee cannot itself mandate implementation.

## Australia

Australia's national PID strategy from 2024 was co-designed under the coordination of the Australian Research Data Commons (ARDC), and is explicitly not a binding, top-down policy[@rep-AustralianNational-24]. It builds on a cost-benefit analysis, commissioned by ARDC and the Australian Access Federation, which estimated that manual rekeying of metadata costs nearly 38,000 person days a year, and recommended 80% adoption of ORCID, ROR, RAiD and DOIs for outputs and grants within five years, with further work on IGSN for samples[@rep-IncentivesInvest-22]. Stakeholder groups support the implementation, and have published guidance for institutions[@rep-PIDsImplementation-26].

## United States

The US proposal for a national PID strategy was published in 2024 by a working group of the Open Research Funders Group (ORFG), using the RDA guide as its framework[@rep-DevelopingUS-24]. It builds on federal policy on public access to research, which asks agencies to make metadata including PIDs publicly available for federally funded research. The proposal recommends standard PIDs for common research entities, such as DOIs for publications, data and software, ROR IDs for organisations and funders, ORCID iDs for researchers and grant DOIs for awards. It is intended to become a US national standard through the National Information Standards Organization (NISO).
