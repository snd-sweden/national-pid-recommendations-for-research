# Common themes

**Who develops the strategy.** Most strategies are led or hosted by national organisations for research infrastructure or open research, such as Jisc, ARDC, CRKN and the Digital Research Alliance of Canada, NORF, the French Committee for Open Science and the Czech National Library of Technology. The Dutch roadmap was developed by a national working group with an advisory board, Finland's by an open expert network, and the US proposal by a funders' working group aiming for a formal national standard. Germany's roadmap comes from a time-limited project, and is to be followed by a national action plan. As the RDA guide notes, most strategies build on existing PID consortia, services or governance groups rather than starting from scratch. All are the result of broad consultation, from the UK's workshops and Canada's workshops with 40 stakeholders to the open co-design of Australia's strategy, released as a draft in 2023. Many also share a consultancy: MoreBrains Cooperative, whose co-founder wrote the UK roadmap, produced the UK, Australian, Irish and Czech cost-benefit analyses, the UK case for investment, the Canadian report of 2022 and, with NORF, the Irish roadmap.

**Which PIDs are prioritised.** ORCID iDs, DOIs and ROR IDs feature in all nine RDA case studies, and recur in every strategy that names PID systems. RAiD for projects and DOIs for grants appear in most of them, but are often at an early stage. Both are prioritised in the United Kingdom, Australia and Ireland, France recommends a national deployment of RAiD, and both are under consideration or still emerging in Canada and the United States. Identifiers for software differ: France recommends SWHID, while the Dutch and US documents recommend DOIs. The Finnish and German roadmaps, like the EOSC PID policy, do not prescribe specific systems, but ask for agreed criteria and guidelines for selecting them. The five priority PIDs used in several countries go back to the UK roadmap's consultations, although in 2019 it still left the choice between ROR and ISNI open; the same five underlie the UK, Australian, Irish and Czech cost-benefit analyses, and the Australian analysis also recommends further work on IGSN for samples.

**Evidence for investment.** The RDA guide names a clear value proposition as a critical component of a national strategy, and cost-benefit analyses are used for this in the United Kingdom, Australia, Ireland and the Czech Republic. These analyses share authors and use the same method, first developed for the United Kingdom. The analyses estimate the time lost to rekeying metadata about publications, grants and projects: nearly 55,000 person days a year in the UK, 38,000 in Australia, more than 4,000 in Ireland and 7,207 in the Czech Republic. The method is refined in each round; the revised UK analysis, for example, uses the Australian survey of how often metadata is rekeyed. The Irish and Czech analyses find that central support makes adoption pay off sooner, even when its own cost is included, and in the UK a government-commissioned review of research bureaucracy endorsed the proposed PID support network in 2022.

**Integration into workflows.** Most strategies aim to integrate PIDs into the systems used for funding, research management and publishing, so that information entered once can be reused, which the French recommendations call "tell us once". The UK's PID-optimised workflows show this step by step, and the Canadian, Dutch and German strategies emphasise automated exchange between local systems and PID registries. The UK roadmap modelled PID-optimised open access workflows as early as 2019. The Australian Research Council's ORCID integration saves researchers up to three or four days per grant application, while the Canadian report found too few integrations and proposed a shared national metadata store.

**Sustainable funding and governance.** Several strategies rely on national consortia for PID systems such as ORCID and DataCite to lower costs and coordinate adoption. The UK case for investment describes three components of a national approach: consortia for access to key PIDs, a coordination body and a dedicated support team; the UK ORCID consortium alone saved an estimated £2.4–9.6 million over six years. Some establish or propose a central support function, such as the Czech National PID Centre, Ireland's central PID support service, the UK's planned PID support network and Germany's central point of contact for PID questions. Several documents also address the risks of depending on PID infrastructures: the French recommendations call for a risk analysis that includes digital sovereignty, and the Knowledge Exchange study and the EOSC PID policy ask PID service providers for transparency about costs and plans for continuity, and the UK roadmap proposed a task force for the sustainability of PID infrastructures.

**Mandates or incentives.** Few strategies rely on mandates. The UK roadmap describes a catch-22: a mandate without useful integrations yields coverage but little benefit, while useful integrations reduce the need for a mandate. The Canadian report notes that mandates have produced empty ORCID records, and advises agreeing on a strategy before choosing tactics such as mandates. Australia's strategy is explicitly not binding, and the US proposal aims for a national standard, while Germany's roadmap is to be followed by a binding action plan.

**Including smaller organisations.** The UK case for investment notes that smaller institutions carry a disproportionate burden in implementing PIDs, and the Canadian report found that many cannot afford integrations. The Irish analysis names integration costs, technical capacity and senior buy-in as barriers, and the Czech analysis recommends PIDs in shared national systems so that smaller organisations also benefit.

**Awareness and competence.** All strategies that describe actions include promotion, training or guidance, ranging from Canada's engagement toolkit and Australia's institutional guides to Finland's, France's and Germany's plans for training. The Canadian report stresses that the benefits of PIDs must be both tangible and effectively communicated.
